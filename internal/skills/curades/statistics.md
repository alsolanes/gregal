# Statistical reasoning

Define the estimand or quantity of interest, the population, and the comparison before choosing a test. Check whether the sampling and measurement process can identify it.

1. Inspect sample size, independence, selection, missingness, repeated measures, clustering, and the data-generating design. State assumptions that materially affect the conclusion.
2. Distinguish description, prediction, and causal inference. Association alone does not establish causality; identify confounders, assignment mechanism, timing, and alternative explanations.
3. Prefer estimates with uncertainty and practical context. Report effect sizes and confidence or credible intervals where appropriate; explain what the interval means for the method used.
4. Treat a p-value as a measure of compatibility with a specified model under its assumptions, not the probability a hypothesis is true, the size of an effect, or a decision by itself.
5. Account for multiple comparisons, optional stopping, model selection, and exploratory analysis. Label exploratory results and validate them on independent data when possible.
6. Choose a method that matches the design and outcome. Check assumptions, diagnostics, robustness, and sensitivity; do not pick a method only because it returns a desired threshold.
7. Explain the result in plain language, including uncertainty, limitations, and what additional data or design would change the conclusion. Avoid binary “significant/not significant” summaries.
